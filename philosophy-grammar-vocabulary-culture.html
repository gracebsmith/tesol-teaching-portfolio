Teaching Philosophy #6

Grammar, Vocabulary, and Culture

Beyond the four major language skills (listening, speaking, reading, and writing), I believe grammar, vocabulary, and culture are also essential parts of language learning and communication. My goal is not just for my students to memorize words or grammar rules, but to use language appropriately, accurately, and meaningfully in real-world scenarios. I view grammar acquisition as a gradual process. Rather than teaching grammar as a set of rules to memorize, I believe students should learn how grammatical forms convey meaning and serve communicative purposes. For vocabulary instruction, I value approaches that help students make connections among words, including collocations, connotations, synonyms, and antonyms. I believe students learn vocabulary best when they encounter and use words repeatedly in meaningful contexts. Likewise, I believe culture instruction should extend beyond visible elements of culture to include values, perspectives, communication styles, and social expectations. Students should have opportunities to examine cultural differences critically and develop intercultural competence.

These principles in classroom instruction and practice are achieved through interactive and communicative activities. Grammar instruction includes a combination of focus-on-form activities (such as fill-in-the-blank worksheets), meaningful practice, and opportunities for output (i.e., allowing them to do role plays using the target grammar principle). I provide feedback through recasts, elicitation, clarification requests, peer correction, and written corrective feedback on their writing samples. To develop students’ vocabulary, I incorporate activities such as word webs, synonym and antonym tasks, flashcards, and partner definition games. For culture instruction, I use critical incidents (“cultural bumps”), authentic materials, current events, films, speech acts, and nonverbal (body) communication. Overall, I aim to create opportunities for students to use grammar, vocabulary, and cultural knowledge together so that they can communicate confidently and appropriately beyond the classroom.

Ultimately, I believe that vocabulary, grammar, and culture should not be taught in isolation. Effective communication requires students to know not only what words and grammatical structures mean, but also when and why they should be used. For this reason, I strive to integrate these components into meaningful communicative tasks. By connecting language instruction to authentic contexts, students can better understand how English is used in real-world interactions and develop confidence in their ability to communicate across a variety of situations.
