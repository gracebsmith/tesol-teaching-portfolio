Teaching Philosophy #1

TESOL Theories

My growing teaching philosophy includes theories that affect day-to-day classes, as well as overarching beliefs about my general teaching practices. I believe in creating a negotiated syllabus with students, centering lessons on course outcomes, and maintaining an affective-humanistic approach toward the treatment of students. In my view, the relationship between teachers and students plays a critical role in classroom success. However, I do not believe that the social climate is more important than materials or methods, as some interpretations of the humanistic approach suggest. Instead, I view the classroom environment, materials, and instructional methods as complementary elements that work together to support learning.

In practice, course and learning outcomes serve as part of the “bones” of a lesson and the overall course structure. I think of them as the bones because they provide the underlying framework that supports all instructional decisions. The outcomes clarify what students should know and be able to do by the end of a lesson or unit, which helps me determine what content to teach, what activities to use, and how learning should be assessed. Without that structure, lessons can become disconnected from larger course goals. At the same time, teachers and students share decision-making in my classroom. I let students see those “bones” and vote on projects or activities that will help them achieve the outcomes. To foster positive professional relationships, I also ask students why they are studying English, such as for school, work, or personal goals, so I can better align instruction with their needs and interests. These beliefs and practices contribute to a positive and purposeful learning environment.

Grace Smith
