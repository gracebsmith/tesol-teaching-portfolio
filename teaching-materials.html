Developing ideas in an admissions essay

This lesson helps students recognize the difference between a general claim and a well-developed idea, then apply supporting details to a paragraph for their admissions essay.

Learning goals

  • Identify developed and underdeveloped paragraphs and explain what is missing.
  • Draft a supporting paragraph using specific details about a personal experience.

Lesson sequence

  1. Warm-up: timed quickwrite about technology and connection.
  2. Notice: compare examples and identify details that strengthen development.
  3. Practice: model questions such as “Why?”, “How?”, and “What does that mean?” before students draft.
  4. Reflect: close with a one-minute explanation of the lesson’s main learning.

Course context

Course: University Prep Writing · Semester: Fall 2026 · Date: Wednesday, October 7, 2026 · Class size: 12

In Week 5, students were preparing a mock or real college admissions essay. After reviewing essay structure and sample essays, this lesson focused on developing ideas because idea development had been an area for growth on earlier writing assignments.

Objectives

  • Students will identify developed ideas and practice developing their own.
  • Students will distinguish well-developed and underdeveloped paragraphs and explain what information is missing.
  • Students will write a developed supporting paragraph for an admissions essay using details about a personal experience or story.

Materials

Chromebooks, a slide presentation, projector, course-site access, an online document, and a name-selection wheel.

Timed lesson sequence

  1. 12:00–12:02 · Opening and attendance. Take attendance. A student leads the opening exercise by sharing a quotation, scripture, or prayer.
  2. 12:02–12:04 · Objectives and agenda. Preview the warm-up, guided skill practice, and independent application. Review the lesson objectives and course outcomes.
  3. Transition · 30 seconds. Invite students to open their quick-write document and allow time for everyone to get ready.
  4. 12:05–12:15 · Quick Write 13. Students write in response to: “Compare and contrast how technology both brings people closer together and makes them feel more isolated.” Monitor writing. If students finish early, invite them to consider how technology use differs in personal, national, and global contexts.
  5. Transition · 30 seconds. Ask students to finish their thought, save their document, and close their computers.
  6. 12:16–12:22 · Notice idea development. Compare “Technology is important for students” with a more specific sentence explaining how technology can support student success. Discuss which gives a clearer picture and what information is missing from the general sentence. Activate prior knowledge about Helen Keller, then share a short background text before reading two paragraphs.
  7. 12:23–12:30 · Compare paragraphs. Read an underdeveloped paragraph and ask students what questions remain. Students discuss with a partner and share questions. Read the more developed paragraph, then discuss which questions it answers.
  8. 12:31–12:38 · Discuss strong development. Students think about weak and strong development, then contribute ideas to a class chart. Highlight details such as dates, locations, explanations, reasons, and examples.
  9. 12:39–12:44 · Teacher model. Introduce revision questions: “Why?”, “How?”, “Like what?”, “When?”, “Where?”, “Who?”, and “What does that mean?” Think aloud while expanding the topic sentence “Learning English can improve career opportunities,” then show a developed example paragraph.
  10. 12:45–1:00 · Independent application. Students draft a supporting paragraph for their admissions essay using the revision questions. Review the assignment prompts and requirements, display them for reference, and circulate to monitor and support students.
  11. Transition · 30 seconds. Bring students’ attention back to the class for closure.
  12. 1:01–1:05 · One-Minute Teacher closure. Students prepare to explain to an absent classmate what the class learned. Use the name-selection wheel to choose a student to share.
  13. Transition · 30 seconds. Remind students of homework and dismiss the class.

Contingency plan and homework

If time remains, students continue their admissions essay draft, checking for a hook, thesis, supporting details, and conclusion while staying within the 1,500-character limit. The lesson plan lists the first admissions-essay draft as due Thursday night and Week 5 typing practice as due Friday night.

Teacher reflection

  • What did I miss that I need to cover next time?
  • Did I help students accomplish the learning objectives?

Peer review for an admissions essay

This checklist guides students as they give a partner focused feedback on an admissions essay draft. It combines reader response with a review of the assignment requirements.

Review criteria

  1. Hook and clear thesis 2 points
  2. Supporting details and suggestions for adding or deleting content 3 points
  3. Conclusion or concluding thought 1 point
  4. First-person or personal pronouns 1 point
  5. Length at or below 1,500 characters 1 point
  6. Complete response to the chosen prompt 1 point
  7. Required formatting 1 point
  8. Grammar and spelling 1 point

Total: 11 points, with space for additional comments.
